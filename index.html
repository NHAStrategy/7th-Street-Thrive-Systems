Where 7th Street Thrives is today, and where it is going.

A thriving Black arts, business, and cultural district that draws on and sustains 7th Street's rich legacy. Four strategies, each moving from what the team does now to what a future entity would do later.

How the plan is built

The point is not just to fix things — it's to change who has the power and responsibility to keep them fixed.

Each strategy starts with goals. Every goal runs through a room where the work actually happens — a City meeting, the Business Council, a permit office, a funder's process — into an activity. Each activity has an owner today and an owner in the future, and a measure that shows whether it worked.

Two kinds of work run through every strategy. Some of it happens inside City of Oakland systems — permits, streetlights, trash pickup — where the City is responsible for delivering and the team's job is to hold it to that. The rest is work the community does itself. Before taking on anything new, the team asks two questions: do we need to be involved, and if yes, do we lead?

NOW
What the team does today

7th Street Thrives, led by East Bay Permanent Real Estate Cooperative (EB PREC) and Bay Area LISC, includes the 7th Street Corridor Business Council, monthly Neighborhood Enhanced Services Team (NEST) meetings, and other projects in partnership with West Oakland Environmental Indicators Project (WOEIP) and the West Oakland Community Collaborative (WOCC).

BRIDGE
The steps in between

The in-between activities that lead to the work a future entity (or collective) will permanently hold: writing a procedure down, signing an agreement, running a pilot, making a decision.

LATER
Who carries the work in the future

A permanent neighborhood organization — possibly a Community Development Corporation (CDC), or a collective with CDC capacity — which can own property, run programs, administer grants and regrants, and hire staff. It works in continued partnership with existing entities (the Business Council, WOCC) and new ones (an Ambassador Program).

01
Strategy one

Core Infrastructure

Running the organization — the money, the staff and the records that let everything else happen.

Organizational Infrastructure

Every initiative needs a back office. Today the grants and the budget sit with LISC, and the small core team and its consultants hold the planning documents: the strategic plan, the fundraising plan, the research on what kind of organization to become, a library of one-page explainers, and a communications strategy.

This is the only strategy with no City work in it. Nothing here depends on a City department; the team can do all of it itself. The goals fall into three groups — funding, operations and knowledge-keeping — with culture and sustainability running through all three.

Culture and sustainability run through every goal here rather than sitting in any one of them.

Strategy two

Build Connections

The relationships the corridor depends on — with the City, with partners, with property owners, with businesses, with funders and with the press.

Direct OrganizingOrganizational InfrastructureMarket Making

Fourteen of this strategy's sixteen goals start with "build a relationship." The first job is to group them and name one person responsible for each group: government, community partners, real estate, business and funders, and media.

Relationships happen in specific rooms: City and council meetings, the Business Council, the City's monthly service meeting, resident engagement, social media and the newsletter, and the funders' own processes. Two rooms do not exist yet: a working link to the City's real estate team, and a network of developers and landowners kept separate from the Business Council.

03
Strategy three

Activate Land

Putting empty lots and storefronts to use, and clearing the obstacles that keep them empty.

Real Estate AdvocacyMarket MakingService Delivery Improvements

7th Street has empty lots and empty ground-floor storefronts along the corridor that should be activated. This strategy includes activating vacant or underutilized land (by buying, or through real estate advocacy), rehabbing empty buildings or storefronts, and connecting businesses and business owners with these opportunities.

"Activate" is the first in a series of steps that leads to permanent use. An event on a vacant lot comes first. If it works, temporary improvements follow — paint, seating, lights — then a short-term tenant, a long-term tenant and eventually a form of ownership. For storefronts the same idea is a master lease: one organization rents the space and sublets it to a small business.

This is the strategy that leans most on the City: permits that last longer than 30 days, a say in the City's long-range land-use plan, and partnership on buying tax-delinquent land. The team's own work is the data — knowing which sites are empty and who owns them — and the activations.

Strategy four

Corridor Character

Keeping the street clean, safe and recognizably itself — and fixing the City systems behind the problems.

Service Delivery ImprovementsDirect OrganizingMarket Making

This is the strategy people can see from the sidewalk: a clean corridor, working streetlights, safe walking, less illegal dumping, and a street that looks and feels like the Black arts and culture business district it has been. It touches more City departments than any other: trash collection, street lighting, capital projects, permits, enforcement and the encampment program.

The team's work includes teaching residents and businesses to file 311 requests, tracking service requests, scoring blighted corners on a regular basis, mapping streetlights, and documenting where dumping happens and when.

The other half is identity: cultural programming and regular rituals that honor local Black arts and culture; storefront facade improvements; and formal recognition of 7th Street as a Black cultural and economic district.
